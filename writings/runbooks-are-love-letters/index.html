Runbooks are love letters

A runbook has exactly one reader: a stranger at 3am who is you. Write to them the way you would want to be met at that hour — briefly, kindly, and with the commands already typed.

Summary

A runbook is a document you write while calm, to be read by someone who is not calm. That the two people share a body is a coincidence of self-hosting, and the worst excuse available. Three-in-the-morning you has the same qualifications as a new hire on their first night shift: technical access, no context, and a strong preference for not being here.

This RFC proposes a simple test for every runbook. Open it at 3am, under artificial light, with something beeping. If any sentence in it makes you do arithmetic, the sentence has failed. If any step assumes you remember why the service exists, the step has failed. A runbook that passes the test is a letter that says: I knew you would be here. I got the lantern ready.

The 3am stranger

Describe them precisely, because you are writing for them and nobody else:

  • They have forgotten the incident from last time, on purpose, as a survival mechanism.
  • They cannot remember what svc-03 does, only that it is named like something important.
  • They will read at approximately double normal speed and retain half as much.
  • They are doing this instead of sleeping, and they know it, and they would like it to be over.

Write for that person and the document is good. Write for the conference-talk version of yourself — the one with diagrams and an architecture philosophy — and you have written a blog post with delusions of usefulness.

What a runbook is not

It is not documentation. Documentation explains a system to someone who wants to understand it. A runbook rescues someone who just wants it to stop. The difference shows up in the verbs: documentation says “the queue drains when…”, a runbook says “run this, then check that”.

It is also not a place for options. At 3am, two options is one too many. If there are genuinely two paths, mark one START HERE and put the other under a heading that begins “only if the above made it worse”.

A modest format

# runbook: thing will not start

WHAT YOU WILL SEE: exit 1, message about "lock"
SEVERITY: annoying, not bleeding

1. ls /var/run/thing/        # leftover pid file?
2. rm /var/run/thing/thing.pid
3. systemctl start thing
4. curl -s localhost:8420/health   # want: {"ok":true}

IF STILL BROKEN: it is the disk. It is always the disk.
df -h /var   # over 90% = your answer

LAST UPDATED: 2026-04-01 (by calmer you)

Four kinds of line, and nothing else: what you will see, what to do, how to know it worked, and where to go next. Anything beyond that is a love letter with a résumé stapled to it.

Closing

Documentation is written for an audience. A runbook is written for a person. The whole genre works because somewhere down the line, the person who has to cope is the one who prepared — and the kindest thing you can send forward through time is not wisdom, not architecture, but a short list of working commands.
